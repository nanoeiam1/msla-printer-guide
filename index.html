Setting up and running the grayscale mSLA printer

A step-by-step guide for anyone in the lab.

2How the system works

Knowing which box does what makes every later step easier to follow, and makes errors easier to read.

Meet the hardware

What each part looks like and where its important bits are. Click a number on a picture to see what that part is for, or hover over an item in a list to find it in the picture.

Raspberry Pi 4

The brain. A small computer the size of a credit card, usually inside a case.

Click a number to see what that part is for, or click the photo to see it larger.

  1. 1USB-C power in. The Pi’s own power supply plugs in here.
  2. 2Two micro-HDMI ports. The LCD’s cable plugs into one of them. HDMI0 is the one next to the power port. Ours uses HDMI0 / HDMI1; the display settings refer to it by number.
  3. 3Ethernet port. The big square socket with a clip. The cable from the laptop goes here.
  4. 4USB ports. The cable to the control board goes into any of these. Blue ones are USB 3.0, black ones USB 2.0.
  5. 5microSD card slot, on the underside. The Pi’s card lives here. Never take it out.
  6. 6Status lights. Red means power. Green flickers while the Pi is busy; wait for it to stop before unplugging.
  7. 740-pin header. Leave anything connected here as it is.

BTT SKR Mini E3 control board

The hands. Pictured: V3.0. Older notes sometimes call this board “the Manta” or “Manta SKR Mini E3”; it’s this SKR Mini E3, not a Manta board.

Click a number to see what that part is for, or click the picture to see it larger.

  1. 1SD card slot. The firmware card goes in here when flashing (section 10). BTT lists a green light, D9, that comes on when a card is inserted.
  2. 2Micro-USB port. The USB cable from the Pi plugs in here.
  3. 3Power input (DC IN). The 12 V supply connects to this screw terminal. The board won’t run from USB alone.
  4. 4Main chip. Its text tells you the revision: STM32G0B1RET6 means V3.0, a chip starting STM32F103 means V2.0.
  5. 5Status and power lights. The power light is on whenever the board has power. The status light blinks while firmware is being installed.
  6. 6Motor connectors (XM, YM, ZAM, ZBM, EM). Our Z motor plugs into ZAM / ZBM.
  7. 7Endstop and thermistor connectors (X, Y and Z STOP, THB, TH0). The Z endstop and the LED temperature sensors plug in along here. Check the printed label next to each one.
  8. 8Fan connectors (FAN0, FAN1, FAN2). Not used: our printer has no fans.
  9. 9High-power outputs (HB and HE0). Screw terminals that switch high-current loads; on mSLA printers the UV LEDs usually connect here. Ours: which LED on which

Masking LCD

The grayscale screen the light shines through. Model: DBM066M04K01, a 6.6-inch 4K mono LCD.

Click a number to see what that part is for, or click the photo to see it larger.

  1. 1LCD panel. The 6.6-inch 4K mono screen that sits under the resin vat. It shows each layer as a grey image while the light shines up through it. It’s thin glass: don’t press on it, and don’t let the build plate crash into it.
  2. 2Panel ribbon and connector. The orange flexible strip built into the panel, with the connector the ribbon cable plugs into. Very fragile: don’t fold it, and only plug or unplug with the power off.
  3. 3Ribbon cable. Carries the picture signal from the driver board to the panel. Keep it flat, don’t crease it, and power off before unplugging it.
  4. 4Joining board. A small board that joins two lengths of ribbon cable in this kit. On our printer: used / not used
  5. 5Driver board. Turns the Pi’s HDMI signal into what the panel needs. This is the board sold with the panel in this photo; ours is a BHTM01-6-6-4K board, which looks different.

3Software

The printer runs on Klipper, and Klipper lives in two places at once.

Klipper is split across two chips

Press play to watch one layer being printed and see which half does each job. Violet marks the Pi’s jobs and green the board’s; the matching line in the cards below lights up too.

Klipper host

Runs on the Raspberry Pi (our mSLA fork)

  • Reads and parses the print files
  • Runs the resin logic
  • Draws each layer image on the LCD
  • Hosts the Mainsail page
USB cable Must be built from the same code

Klipper MCU firmware

Runs on the SKR Mini E3’s chip

  • Keeps precise timing
  • Moves the Z motor
  • Switches the UV LEDs
  • Reads the endstop and sensors
Firmware built on our PiComes from the same Klipper code as the host, so the two halves match and Klipper starts.
BTT’s ready-made firmware.binDates from March 2022 and doesn’t match our fork, so Klipper refuses to start. Don’t use it.

That’s why section 10 builds the board’s firmware on the Pi itself, from the Klipper code the Pi already has.

5Why we connect through a laptop

Normally you would reach Mainsail over the lab Wi-Fi, but public Wi-Fi doesn’t let devices see each other. So we plug the Pi straight into a laptop and turn on Windows Internet Connection Sharing (ICS). The laptop then acts as a small router: it gives the Pi an address in the 192.168.137.x range and passes internet through to it. This has to be set up at the start of every session.

6Connect to Mainsail (every session)

This is the first thing to do every session: Tera Term, WinSCP and everything else in this guide start from here. You share the laptop’s Wi-Fi through its Ethernet port (Windows calls this Internet Connection Sharing, or ICS), power up the printer, find the Pi’s address and open Mainsail. It takes about five minutes once you’ve done it a couple of times.

Step 1: Share the laptop’s Wi-Fi through the Ethernet port (ICS)

  1. Check the laptop is on Wi-Fi and the internet works. That’s the connection you’re about to share with the Pi.
  2. Open Network Connections. Press Windows + R to open the Run box, type ncpa.cpl and press Enter.
  3. Open the Wi-Fi properties. In the Network Connections window, right-click Wi-Fi (not Ethernet) and choose Properties.
  4. Go to the Sharing tab.
  5. Share the Wi-Fi through the Ethernet port. Tick Allow other network users to connect through this computer’s Internet connection. Then, under Home networking connection, choose the Ethernet adapter the Pi plugs into. It’s usually called “Ethernet”, or “Ethernet 2” if you use a USB adapter.
  6. Click OK. If Windows warns that the LAN adapter will be set to 192.168.137.1, click Yes.
Share from Wi-Fi, not Ethernet

The box you tick is in the Wi-Fi adapter’s properties, because Wi-Fi is the connection being shared. If you ticked it on the Ethernet adapter by mistake, untick it there and redo the steps on Wi-Fi.

Step 2: Plug in and power up

  1. Check the cables while everything is off. The Ethernet cable goes from the laptop to the Raspberry Pi’s Ethernet port. (The SKR Mini E3 has no network port. Older notes say “connect the Manta via Ethernet”; that was the old setup.) The USB cable goes from the Pi to the control board. The HDMI cable from the Pi to the LCD is already connected; leave it alone.
  2. Plug in the 12 V supply for the control board. A red power light on the board should come on.
  3. Plug in the Pi’s power. The red light on the Pi comes on and the green light flickers while it boots. Give it one to two minutes.

Step 3: Find Mainsail’s address and open it

Mainsail runs on the Pi, so to open it you need the Pi’s address on the shared network. It can change between sessions, so look it up every time with arp -a.

  1. Open Command Prompt. Press Windows, type cmd and press Enter.
  2. List the devices the laptop can see. Type this and press Enter: 
    arp -a
    Shortcut: arp -a -N 192.168.137.1 shows only the shared Ethernet network, so there’s less to read.
  3. Find the right block. The output is split into blocks, one per network connection. Look for the block headed Interface: 192.168.137.1, which is the Ethernet connection you shared. It looks something like this: 
    Interface: 10.110.42.17 --- 0x9                     <-- your Wi-Fi: skip this block
  Internet Address      Physical Address      Type
  10.110.32.1           00-00-0c-9f-f0-01     dynamic
  10.110.63.255         ff-ff-ff-ff-ff-ff     static

Interface: 192.168.137.1 --- 0x11                    <-- shared Ethernet: look here
  Internet Address      Physical Address      Type
  192.168.137.84        dc-a6-32-xx-xx-xx     dynamic   <-- the Pi, so Mainsail
  192.168.137.255       ff-ff-ff-ff-ff-ff     static    <-- ignore: broadcast
  224.0.0.22            01-00-5e-00-00-16     static    <-- ignore: multicast
  239.255.255.250       01-00-5e-7f-ff-fa     static    <-- ignore: multicast

    Example output. Your numbers will differ.

  4. Spot the Pi. Use these clues:
    • Only look under Interface: 192.168.137.1. Other blocks belong to the laptop’s other connections, such as Wi-Fi.
    • 192.168.137.1 is the laptop, not the Pi. It’s the address in the block’s heading.
    • Pick a 192.168.137.x line marked dynamic. “Dynamic” means a real device that got its address from the laptop. Lines marked static are Windows’ own entries.
    • Ignore .255, and anything starting 224 or 239. Those are broadcast and multicast addresses, not devices.
    • Check the Physical Address (MAC). A Raspberry Pi’s usually starts with dc-a6-32, e4-5f-01, d8-3a-dd, 2c-cf-67 or b8-27-eb. Ours is fill in; once it’s written here, matching it is the surest check.
    • There’s usually only one. With just the Pi plugged in, you’ll normally see a single dynamic 192.168.137.x line, often the first one listed.
  5. Open Mainsail. In a web browser, type http:// followed by the address you found (for example http://192.168.137.84) and press Enter. The Mainsail dashboard loads.
  6. If several addresses looked right, try each one in the browser. The one that loads Mainsail is the Pi.
  7. If no 192.168.137.x line showed up, wait another minute and run arp -a again. The Pi only appears once it has finished booting and asked the laptop for an address.
  8. Write the address in the lab log (appendix A). You can bookmark the page, but check the address again next session. The shortcut http://mainsail.local may also work; if it doesn’t, use the number.

If it doesn’t connect

Work down this list in order, checking Mainsail again after each step.

  1. Check the cable. Make sure it clicks into both ports and the lights next to the Pi’s Ethernet port are lit or blinking.
  2. Wait and retry. Give the Pi two full minutes after power-on, then run arp -a again.
  3. Restart ICS. Go back to the Wi-Fi adapter’s Properties → Sharing, untick the first box and click OK. Then open it again, tick the box, pick the Ethernet adapter and click OK.
  4. Power-cycle the Pi. If you can’t reach the Pi at all, unplugging it is the only option: unplug its power, wait 10 seconds, plug it back in and wait two minutes. (When you can reach it, always shut it down properly instead.)
  5. Check the basics on the laptop. Is Wi-Fi actually connected to the internet? Is a VPN turned on? Turn VPNs off.
  6. Check the browser. Type http://, not https://. Try a different browser.
  7. Try a different Ethernet cable or adapter.

7Log in to the Pi with Tera Term

Tera Term opens a text-only command window into the Pi over the network (this is called SSH). You need it to build firmware and change system files. It is only a window: the Pi does the actual work.

  1. Complete section 6 so you know the Pi’s IP address.
  2. Open Tera Term. A New connection window appears. (If it doesn’t, use File → New connection.)
  3. Select TCP/IP. In Host, type the Pi’s IP (for example 192.168.137.84). Set Service to SSH, TCP port to 22, and SSH version to SSH2. Click OK.
  4. The first time, a Security Warning about an unknown host key appears. This is normal. Tick the box to add the machine to the known hosts list and click Continue. It may appear again if the Pi’s IP changes.
  5. In the SSH Authentication window, enter user name biqu and passphrase biqu, make sure Use plain password to log in is selected, and click OK.
  6. You’re in when you see a prompt ending in $, like this: 
    biqu@mainsail:~ $

Tips for the command line

  • When a command asks for a password (for example after sudo), nothing appears as you type. That’s normal; type biqu and press Enter.
  • Copy in Tera Term by selecting text with the mouse. Paste by right-clicking. Ctrl + C does not copy here; it stops the running command.
  • Commands are case-sensitive and spaces matter. Copy them from this guide rather than retyping.
  • Press Tab to auto-complete file and folder names.

8Move files with WinSCP

WinSCP shows the laptop’s files on the left and the Pi’s files on the right. You drag files between them.

  1. Open WinSCP. The Login window opens with New Site selected.
  2. Set File protocol to SFTP. Type the Pi’s IP in Host name, leave Port number as 22, and enter user name biqu and password biqu.
  3. Optional: click Save and name it “mSLA Pi” so you don’t retype it next time. If the IP changes, edit the saved site.
  4. Click Login. Accept the host key warning the first time (click Yes or Accept).
  5. The right pane opens in /home/biqu on the Pi. Drag files between the panes. Press F5 to refresh if a new file doesn’t show up.

Backup routine

Before changing any configuration, drag the whole /home/biqu/printer_data/config folder to the laptop and rename the copy with today’s date, for example config-2026-10-19. If something breaks, you can drag the old printer.cfg back.

9Use Mainsail

Mainsail is the printer’s control page. Everything you do day to day (checking status, sending commands, editing the config, starting prints) happens here. Button names can differ slightly between Mainsail versions.

The parts of the page

Restart options, and when to use each

  • Klipper restart reloads printer.cfg. Use it after editing the config.
  • Firmware restart also resets the connection to the control board. Use it after an emergency stop or after reflashing the board. You can also type FIRMWARE_RESTART in the console.
  • Reboot restarts the whole Pi. Shutdown turns it off safely. Wait for the Pi’s green light to stop flickering before you unplug it.

Edit printer.cfg

  1. Back up the config folder first (section 8).
  2. Click Machine, then click printer.cfg. An editor opens.
  3. Make your change. Lines starting with # are comments and are ignored.
  4. Click Save & Restart. If Klipper reports an error, the message usually names the section and line. Fix it, or put the backup back.

Read errors and ask for help

Errors show up as a red banner on the dashboard and in the console. The full detail is in klippy.log (in Machine, under the logs folder, or in ~/printer_data/logs/). When you ask for help, send klippy.log and a screenshot of the banner. Typing STATUS in the console prints Klipper’s current state.

Test commands

UV light will switch on

Run these only when the board is flashed, printer.cfg is complete and the LCD works. Take the vat out, wear UV-blocking glasses, and keep a hand near the emergency stop.

CommandWhat you should see

Homing the Z axis

Don’t run G28 Z (home) until the Z motor values in printer.cfg are set and checked. A wrongly configured Z axis can drive the build plate into the LCD. The fork’s author recommends calibrating Z with the vat in place and using the tmc_safe_home option, which lowers the motor current while homing so the plate stalls gently instead of pressing hard.

10Build and flash the control board firmware

The SKR Mini E3 arrives without Klipper on it, so we build Klipper firmware on the Pi and load it into the board with an SD card. It only needs to be done when the board is new, replaced, or the Pi’s Klipper code changes.

Why an SD card and not Tera Term

On the old Manta M4P we flashed through the Pi’s command line because our SD card wasn’t compatible. That route only works on a board that already runs Klipper. A fresh SKR Mini E3 has to get its first Klipper firmware from an SD card, and Klipper’s own notes for this board say the command-line make flash doesn’t work on it.

The whole process at a glance

  1. Build on the Pimake creates klipper.bin; copy it as firmware.bin.
  2. Copy to the laptopDrag it across in WinSCP.
  3. Put it on the cardFAT32 card, top level, named exactly firmware.bin.
  4. Flash the boardCard in, 12 V on, red light blinks. Wait 30 s.
  5. Check the cardRenamed to FIRMWARE.CUR? It worked.

Step 1: Find out which board revision we have

  1. With the power off, look at the board (see the board map). The revision is printed on it, for example SKR MINI E3 V3.0.
  2. Double-check with the text on the main square chip: STM32G0B1RET6 means V3.0; a chip starting STM32F103 means V2.0.
  3. Write it here: V2.0 or V3.0. Every later step depends on it.

Step 2: Prepare the firmware SD card

  1. Use a card of 32 GB or less; 4–8 GB is the most reliable. Not the Pi’s card.
  2. Put it in the laptop’s card reader. Open File Explorer → This PC, right-click the card and choose Format….
  3. Set File system to FAT32 and Allocation unit size to 4096 bytes. Leave Quick Format ticked. Click Start, then OK. This erases the card.
  4. If FAT32 isn’t offered, the card is bigger than 32 GB. Use a smaller card.

Step 3: Build the firmware on the Pi

  1. Log in with Tera Term (section 7).
  2. Move into the Klipper folder: 
    cd ~/klipper
  3. Optional but recommended: read the official settings for this board straight from our own copy of Klipper. Use the v3.0 file for V3.0 or the v2.0 file for V2.0. If what it says disagrees with the table below, trust the file. 
    head -15 ~/klipper/config/generic-bigtreetech-skr-mini-e3-v3.0.cfg
  4. Clear out any old build, then open the settings menu: 
    make clean
make menuconfig
  5. A text menu opens. Use ↑ ↓ to move, Enter to open an option and pick a value, Space to tick a [ ] box, and Esc to go back. Set the values from the table below.
  6. Press Q to quit, then Y to save.
  7. Build it. This takes one to three minutes: 
    make
  8. Check that the file was made. You should see one line with a size and date: 
    ls -l ~/klipper/out/klipper.bin
  9. Copy it to your home folder under the name the board looks for: 
    cp ~/klipper/out/klipper.bin ~/firmware.bin

The bootloader offset must be right

A wrong offset gives firmware that loads but never starts. If you aren’t sure of the revision, stop here and confirm it first.

Step 4: Copy firmware.bin to the laptop

  1. Open WinSCP and log in (section 8). Press F5 so the right pane refreshes.
  2. Find firmware.bin in /home/biqu and drag it to the laptop’s Desktop in the left pane.

Step 5: Put it on the firmware card

  1. Turn on file extensions so you can see the real file name. Windows 11: File Explorer → View → Show → File name extensions. Windows 10: File Explorer → View tab → tick File name extensions.
  2. Copy firmware.bin onto the card’s top level, not inside a folder.
  3. Check the name is exactly firmware.bin, all lowercase. Not firmware.bin.bin, not firmware (1).bin, not klipper.bin. Delete any other files on the card.
    ✓firmware.bin ✗firmware.bin.bin ✗firmware (1).bin ✗Firmware.BIN ✗klipper.bin ✗somefolder\firmware.bin
  4. Right-click the card in File Explorer and choose Eject before pulling it out.

Step 6: Flash the board

  1. Turn the board completely off: unplug the 12 V supply and the USB cable.
  2. Push the firmware card into the SKR Mini E3’s card slot (number 1 on the board map) until it sits fully in.
  3. Plug in the 12 V supply. On the V3.0, BTT says the green SD light (D9) comes on when a card is inserted, and the red status light blinks while the update runs. The update takes about 10 seconds.
  4. Wait a full 30 seconds to be safe. Don’t touch anything.
  5. Unplug the 12 V supply, take the card out and put it back in the laptop.
  6. Look at the file on the card. If it is now called FIRMWARE.CUR, the board accepted it. If it is still firmware.bin, the board didn’t read it; go to the troubleshooting table below.

Step 7: Connect the board to the Pi and confirm

  1. Leave the firmware card out of the board. Plug the USB cable from the Pi into the board, and plug in the 12 V supply.
  2. In Tera Term, type: 
    ls /dev/serial/by-id/*
  3. You should get one line like the one below. On a V3.0 it contains stm32g0b1xx; on a V2.0, stm32f103. 
    /dev/serial/by-id/usb-Klipper_stm32g0b1xx_XXXXXXXXXXXXXXXXXXXXXXXX-if00
  4. Select the whole line in Tera Term (that copies it). Write it here too: board serial ID
  5. In Mainsail, open Machine → printer.cfg. Find the [mcu] section and set the serial: line to the path you copied: 
    [mcu]
serial: /dev/serial/by-id/usb-Klipper_stm32g0b1xx_XXXXXXXXXXXXXXXXXXXXXXXX-if00
  6. Click Save & Restart, then type FIRMWARE_RESTART in the console. If the “Unable to connect” error goes away, the board is talking to the Pi. If Klipper then reports errors about pins, the pin names in printer.cfg don’t match this board. Check them against Klipper’s example config for the SKR Mini E3 (step 3 shows where it is).

Troubleshooting the flash

What you seeLikely causeWhat to try

11Rebuild the Pi from scratch

Only for a new Pi, or if the Pi’s card has died. This is the one time you handle the Pi’s SD card.

This erases the card completely

Use a new or spare card. If the old Pi still works at all, back up /home/biqu/printer_data/config with WinSCP first (section 8) and contact the project owner before going further.

Part A: Write MainsailOS to the card

  1. Install Raspberry Pi Imager (section 4).
  2. Put the new microSD card in the laptop’s card reader.
  3. Open Imager. Under Device, choose Raspberry Pi 4.
  4. Under Operating System, choose Other specific-purpose OS → 3D printing → Mainsail OS.
  5. Under Storage, choose your SD card. Check the size matches the card. Picking the wrong drive erases it.
  6. Fill in the customisation settings. In newer versions of Imager these are steps in the wizard; in older versions, click Next and then Edit settings.
    • Hostname: mainsail
    • Username: biqu, password: biqu
    • SSH (remote access): turn it on and choose password authentication, so Tera Term can log in
    • Wi-Fi: leave blank, because we use Ethernet
    • Localisation: leave blank
  7. Click Write and confirm that you want to erase the card. Writing and verifying takes several minutes. Wait for the “finished” message, then eject the card.
  8. With the Pi powered off, push the card into the Pi’s slot (on the underside). From now on it stays there.
  9. Connect as in section 6. Mainsail should load; errors about the printer config or the MCU are normal at this stage.
If Imager doesn’t show customisation for MainsailOS

Some Imager versions only offer it for certain operating systems. Don’t guess the login. Check the default login in MainsailOS’s own documentation, then add the biqu user or change these instructions to match.

Part B: Install the klipper-mSLA fork

These steps follow the fork author’s instructions. The Pi needs internet for this, which it gets through ICS. Log in with Tera Term and run each block, waiting for the $ prompt before the next.

  1. Update the system. This can take a while: 
    sudo apt update && sudo apt full-upgrade -y
  2. Stop Klipper, keep the stock copy as a backup, and download the fork (branch msla) in its place. The fork’s author says replacing only the klippy folder is enough, but replacing the whole folder does no harm. 
    sudo systemctl stop klipper
cd ~
mv klipper klipper-stock
git clone -b msla https://github.com/sn4k3/klipper.git klipper
  3. Install the image library the fork needs, then reboot: 
    ~/klippy-env/bin/pip install pillow
sudo reboot
  4. Log back in and install the mSLA macros into the config folder: 
    cd ~/printer_data/config
git clone https://github.com/sn4k3/klipper-msla-macros.git
  5. Copy the backed-up printer.cfg back into /home/biqu/printer_data/config/ with WinSCP.
  6. Set up the display (Part C below). Klipper won’t start until the display settings match.
  7. Rebuild and reflash the control board firmware (section 10), because the Klipper code on the Pi has changed.

Part C: Set up the display

The klipper-mSLA fork checks the display settings in two places, the [msla_display] section of printer.cfg and the Pi’s config.txt, and Klipper won’t start until they agree. Our panel is the DBM066M04K01, a 6.6-inch 4K mono LCD with 4098 × 2560 pixels.

A mono panel packs three of its pixels into each pixel the Pi sends, so the Pi’s picture is one-third of the panel’s width. The fork’s example panel is 3840 × 2400, and its config.txt sets the Pi’s picture to 1280 × 2400.

To see the picture size the Pi is producing right now, and where config.txt lives:

# which framebuffers exist (usually just /dev/fb0)
ls /dev/fb*
# the picture size the Pi is producing
cat /sys/class/graphics/fb0/virtual_size
# bookworm or newer keeps config.txt in /boot/firmware/
grep VERSION_CODENAME /etc/os-release

Back up config.txt before editing it:

sudo cp /boot/firmware/config.txt /boot/firmware/config.txt.bak

The fork’s full example settings are on its display page for the TM089CFSP01. They’re for a different panel, so use them as a pattern, not as-is.

BGlossary

mSLA
Masked stereolithography. UV light shines through an LCD “mask” to cure liquid resin one layer at a time.
Klipper
The open-source software that runs the printer. Split between the Pi (host) and the control board (MCU firmware).
klipper-mSLA fork
sn4k3’s modified Klipper that adds resin-printer support, such as drawing layer images on the LCD.
klipper-msla-macros
Ready-made command sequences for resin printing (start, end, pause, LED and display tests).
Mainsail
The web page you use to control the printer.
Moonraker
The background service on the Pi that connects Mainsail to Klipper. You rarely touch it directly.
MainsailOS
A ready-to-flash operating system for the Pi with Klipper, Moonraker and Mainsail preinstalled.
MCU
Microcontroller unit: the STM32 chip on the control board.
Firmware
The compiled program that runs on the MCU (firmware.bin).
Bootloader
A small factory program on the MCU that reads firmware.bin from an SD card and installs it.
menuconfig
The text menu where you choose the board’s chip and settings before building firmware.
printer.cfg
The text file describing how this printer is wired and configured.
config.txt
The Pi’s boot settings file, including HDMI and display resolution.
Framebuffer
The picture the Pi sends out over HDMI.
EDID
Information a screen sends to the Pi describing what resolutions it supports.
ICS
Internet Connection Sharing: a Windows feature that lets the laptop share its Wi-Fi with a device on its Ethernet port.
IP address
A device’s number on the network, for example 192.168.137.84.
MAC address
A fixed hardware ID for a network port, for example dc-a6-32-…. Unlike the IP, it never changes.
arp -a
A Windows command that lists devices the laptop has recently talked to, with their IP and MAC addresses.
SSH
A secure way to open a command line on another computer over the network.
Tera Term
The Windows program we use for SSH into the Pi.
WinSCP and SFTP
WinSCP is the Windows program for copying files to and from the Pi; SFTP is the file-transfer method it uses.
FAT32
An SD card format the board’s bootloader can read. exFAT and NTFS won’t work.
